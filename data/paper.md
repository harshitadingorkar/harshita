# The Generative Power of Props
## A sociomaterial and Actor–Network reading of a low-budget play

> “Actors themselves make everything, including their own frames, their own theories, their own contexts, their own metaphysics, even their own ontologies.”[^1]

### Scene 1: Enter the Cardboard Crown

The cardboard crown is not the kind of object theatre usually asks us to notice. It is not a grand machine hidden beneath the stage, nor an elaborate illusion whose success depends on remaining invisible. It is a plainly limited prop, one of several pieces of the production whose material modesty could have been treated as a shortcoming. In this play, however, the limits did not stay outside the story. The play was a comedy-adventure, a feel-good story about a writer in an existential crisis. Characters he had discarded were stuck in limbo; they needed him to write a play in which each could have a story and a purpose. As he wrote within the play, their search for purpose became entangled with his own. The form already made the production unusually hospitable to its own imperfections: a mistake or a poor prop could be understood as something the writer had put into a low-budget comedy.

That arrangement matters because it changed what counted as an interruption. A prop that looked makeshift did not simply contradict the fiction. It could become evidence of the fiction’s premise: here were characters and performers living inside a story whose author was still trying to find his way. The play was self-aware, but this self-awareness was not only a script device. It became a practical resource for the people making the performance. As the work developed with the available actors, their accents, appearances, and strengths became part of how the characters were written and played. A nearly nonexistent budget was also part of those conditions. The resulting production was not simply a pre-existing script staged with fewer resources; it was a script and performance being adjusted through those resources.

The distinction between a limitation and a creative element is therefore not settled in advance. It is made in practice. A crown can be a sign of royalty, a visible reminder of what the production could not afford, or a cue for a joke about the very idea of a British warrior queen played by an Indian woman. Those meanings are not mutually exclusive. They gather around the same object and become available at different moments. The prop’s theatrical work is not exhausted by what it represents inside the story; it also participates in the production’s commentary on how the story is being made.

This chapter follows that movement from a cardboard crown to the falling dragon head. It asks what changes when a prop is treated not as a passive tool but as a participant in performance. Sociomaterial thinking helps describe how the actor, costume, script, and expectations are made meaningful together; Actor–Network Theory helps trace how that immediate relation is shaped by a wider set of people, objects, resources, and judgments. The argument is not that a cheap object possesses human intention. It is that the object can make a difference: it can invite, constrain, and redirect action, and those changes can become part of the play’s meaning.

### Scene 2: The Head Falls

The event at the center of the analysis is small enough to miss if we are looking only for plot. During a scene, the cardboard dragon head—the box-like prop that gives the narrator a temporary dragon role—falls off. The prop is described as flimsy because it was cheap and needed to be portable. The fall is not an abstract example of theatrical mishap; it is the moment the working notes return to repeatedly. The actor picks up the head, acknowledges the failure as an intentional mistake for laughs, puts it back on, and resumes the scene. No single line of dialogue is needed to understand the change. The action itself shows how performance continues by incorporating an event that the prop has made difficult to ignore.

The scene gives the actor more than one position from which to act. For most of the play, this performer is the narrator. A prop temporarily affords another role: with the dragon head on, the actor is also the dragon. At the same time, there remains a person who can think through the practical problem of keeping the scene from becoming embarrassing. When the head falls, the identities do not switch like costumes laid neatly on a rail. The event presses them together. The performer must deal with the physical object, preserve the narrator’s relation to the story, and decide how the interruption can be made legible to an audience. The prop does not make that decision, but its material failure changes the decision that has to be made.

This is why describing the fall as merely an error is insufficient. “Error” assumes a standard sequence and then measures the event against it. That description is useful, but it cannot explain what the error becomes after it occurs. The head’s fall creates a practical demand; the actor’s response turns that demand into a comic possibility; the play’s meta-theatrical frame gives the response somewhere to belong. The notes describe the actor improvising dialogue that calls attention to the mistake, putting the prop back on, and returning to the scene. The change is not a movement from failure to perfect control. It is a movement from one arrangement of action to another, in which the prop’s failure is allowed to remain present as part of the performance.

The moment also makes visible the prop’s power to grant and withdraw a role. While it is worn, it helps establish the dragon. When it falls, the character’s material support is briefly absent, and the performer must find a way to continue without pretending that nothing happened. This is not a claim that the cardboard has a mind or intention. It is a claim about consequence: the object’s weight, attachment, portability, and instability shape what can happen next. The head becomes an actor in the analytical sense because its material state changes the course of action. That is a modest definition of agency, but it is precisely the one needed here.

### Scene 3: The Actor Behind the Character

The dragon-head incident is legible because it exposes layers that theatrical convention usually asks an audience to hold together without inspecting. There is the actor as a person, the narrator as a character within the play, and the temporary dragon role made available by the prop. The working notes call attention to the actor’s logical, problem-solving self as well as the narrator and dragon. When the prop falls, the performer must move among these positions quickly: to notice the event as a person, to keep the narrator’s scene moving, and to decide whether the dragon can be resumed. The identities are not simply stacked, one behind another. They are enacted in relation to the object and to the scene.

The discussion of performance and identity in the chat offers a useful phrase: “identity is not a priori given but it is practiced.”[^2] In this production, a role is not only a character description printed in a script. It is also made through a body, a voice, an object, and the way these elements are taken up by others. The dragon head does not merely decorate an already-complete dragon. It helps produce the conditions under which the performer can be read as one. When it falls, the relation that helped make that identity is interrupted, and the performer has to construct another route back into the scene.

The same kind of co-production appears in the queen’s role. The notes describe an Indian actress playing a British warrior queen, with the character joking about being a British queen with an Indian accent. A later draft names Queen Yasmine and describes her movement from worrying about her kingdom to questioning the author. The casting choice and the character’s speech do not simply sit beside one another as an actor’s biography and a fictional role. They meet in the performance and create a comic, self-aware layer that the production could use. The point is not that the actor’s identity mechanically determines the character, but that casting, accent, appearance, writing, and audience expectation become material for making the scene.

Other characters make the same process visible in smaller ways. The tree complains about a poor costume; the explorer jokes about a plastic bow and arrow and about the low budget. The actors brought their own strengths and ways of speaking to characters who already had backstories, and the characters were altered and enriched through that adaptation. These examples matter because they show that the dragon head is not an isolated exception. The production repeatedly lets its means of representation enter the representation itself. The prop, actor, and story can be discussed from within the play, so an apparent mismatch becomes a means of making meaning rather than a problem that must be concealed.

### Scene 4: Material Does Not Wait Offstage

Sociomaterial analysis starts from the difficulty of separating what is “social” from what is “material” in a moment like this. The actor’s choices matter, but they are not made apart from the object. The prop matters, but its significance is not fixed by cardboard alone. The role, the gesture, the script, and the audience’s expectations are enacted together. Scott and Orlikowski’s formulation in the chat is that “Material-discursive practices enact specific local resolutions to ontological questions about the nature of phenomena,” and that those resolutions are not fixed in advance but enacted in practice.[^3] The phrase is dense, but the stage gives it a concrete scale: what the dragon is, what the actor is doing, and whether the fall belongs to the fiction are questions answered through the unfolding scene.

This does not mean every material-social relation is harmonious. The head’s flimsiness can frustrate the intended action. Its portability requirement and low cost help explain why it is fragile, but those constraints do not guarantee a comic outcome. They establish conditions under which the event becomes possible. The actor still has to respond, and the response has to be understandable within the play. Material agency is not a substitute for human action; it is a way of noticing that human action is already entangled with objects, spaces, and available resources. The performer does not first act alone and then use a prop. The prop helps establish which actions can be recognized as the dragon’s, and the failure forces a renegotiation of that arrangement.

Ramiller’s description of the material and social as “constitutively entangled in everyday life” makes a related point.[^4] In the play, the low-budget prop and the social meaning of “low budget” are not two separate facts that happen to coincide. The cardboard is material; the joke about the production’s means is social and discursive; the play’s self-awareness lets one become a resource for the other. The phrase “we can say that the writer wrote us into a low-budget comedy” is more than a convenient explanation after an accident. It describes a way the production made its conditions available for performance.

The layered identity of the actor is also a posthumanist question, in the limited sense explored in the working notes: the human performer is not treated as self-contained, with objects serving only as external instruments. A person is “full of things,” as the chat quotes through Ramiller, and identity can emerge through material entanglements rather than precede them.[^5] The dragon head does not erase the actor, nor does it wholly define him. Instead, it participates in the shifting relation among actor, narrator, dragon, stage, and audience. When the head falls, the event changes that relation. The performance is not only social communication; it is also material action.

### Scene 5: A Network of Necessary Things

Actor–Network Theory widens the view from the immediate performer–prop encounter. The dragon head sits among the narrator, the actor, the script, the director, the budget, the competition setting, the stage, the audience, and the judges. The notes explicitly ask how these actors influence one another and where power lies: the budget shapes the prop; the competition’s location makes portability important; the director’s vision depends on both available money and what the props permit; the actor must answer to the director, the audience, and the judges. This is not a claim that each element has equal influence. It is a way to resist explaining the performance through one human intention alone.

The head’s material properties translate one set of conditions into another. A low budget becomes a limited construction choice; portability makes a light, manageable object useful; the same lightness and flimsiness make it vulnerable to falling. Viktorelius is invoked in the chat to describe socio-material arrangements and a “chain of translations.”[^6] Here the translation is not a neat sequence in which a budget line directly causes a joke. It is a series of adjustments: resource limits shape the object; the object’s behavior changes what the actor must do; the play-within-a-play makes self-reference available; the actor’s improvisation can then turn a break in the scene into a comedic moment.

Cresswell and colleagues describe networks of human and non-human actors as interacting to achieve effects through the transformation of their constituent actors.[^7] The fall makes that mutual change easy to see. The prop is no longer only a costume element; the actor is no longer only performing a planned role; the scene is no longer only the sequence written beforehand. The network changes as the event is taken up. The actor’s response changes what the prop means, and the prop’s failure changes the actor’s available performance. Neither side remains untouched by the relation.

Power in this network is uneven. Financial constraints set conditions for what can be built. The director’s vision guides the production, but it is vulnerable to the resources and objects through which that vision must be realized. The audience brings expectations about what a play should conceal or reveal, while judges hold authority to evaluate the work in its competition context. The stage itself affords and limits movement. The head can grant a character identity, but its failure can withdraw that support. These relations do not reduce to a single hierarchy. They overlap: money shapes materials; materials shape performance; performance addresses audiences and judges; their expectations become part of what the actors must navigate.

The more distant rehearsal setting belongs to this network too, though not as the center of the analysis. The working notes describe weekend practice at a friend’s house, where the friend owned a famous restaurant and provided free breakfast. The breakfast motivated the group to practice more. The account is tentative about whether this detail belongs in the paper, and that uncertainty is analytically useful. A network can extend indefinitely; the task is not to include every relation but to show why a relation matters to the event under study. The practice space and meal are relevant as an example of how creative work depends on arrangements outside the script and stage. They need not be made into a direct cause of the dragon head’s fall or of the production’s success.

### Scene 6: When the Play Writes Back

The production’s meta-theatrical structure lets material constraints travel back into the script. The writer inside the play is trying to give discarded characters stories and purposes; the real performers are also working with the actors, props, accents, and resources they have. That resemblance does not collapse the writer into the actual playwright or make every backstage condition part of the fiction. Rather, it gives the production a bridge between levels. A weak prop can be treated as an object in the scene and as evidence of the writer’s imagined low-budget production. A performer can make a joke about the prop and, at the same time, preserve the fictional world by making that joke belong to it.

This is the transformation that the combined framework helps clarify. Sociomaterial analysis shows how the prop and performer become meaningful together, how the head can afford a role and then disturb it, and how a material failure can become an identity event. ANT makes the surrounding relations visible: budget, portability, director, competition, audience, judges, script, and stage. One lens alone could make the moment seem either too small or too diffuse. A close sociomaterial account might understate the wider distribution of power that makes a flimsy portable prop likely. A network account might trace the many connections but miss how the performer experiences and enacts the shift between person, narrator, and dragon. Together, they let the same event be read at more than one scale.

This combined reading also guards against a celebratory shortcut. It would be easy to say that constraints are always good, that any mistake can be turned into art, or that low budgets reliably produce originality. The event does not support those universal claims. A prop falling can interrupt a performance; whether that interruption becomes productive depends on the particular arrangement of script, actor, material, and expectations. The working notes say that mistakes could help the comedy, not that every mistake did so. The head’s fall was useful because the actor could acknowledge it, the play could absorb self-reference, and the performance could continue. The creative possibility was real, but it was contingent.

The same caution applies to the production’s win. The notes say that the play won its competition, a fact worth including briefly because it locates the work within an evaluative setting. The win does not prove that the fallen head caused success, nor does it make every material weakness a virtue. It does show that this was not a private rehearsal exercise alone: the production was performed where judges and audience expectations mattered. In that context, the decision to make the failure legible rather than hide it had stakes. The production’s outcome can sit beside the analysis without being used as a causal shortcut.

### Scene 7: Beyond the Stage

The lesson travels beyond theatre only if it remains specific. In design, education, or organizational work, people also act through materials that can constrain, invite, or redirect practice. A prototype, a form, a room, a software interface, or a budget can participate in shaping what people do. The theatre case does not offer a recipe for turning every constraint into a clever solution. It offers a question: what relations make a constraint operative, and what changes when participants respond to it rather than treating it as background? That question keeps attention on both the immediate encounter and the wider network that gives it force.

There is an apt pun in this example. Theatre gives us actors onstage; Actor–Network Theory asks us to notice actors beyond the human performers. The wordplay should not blur the distinction, but it can help us remember the argument: the performer was not acting alone, and the prop was not merely waiting to be used. The cardboard crown and the fallen dragon head were not authors of the play. They were part of what made this play possible in the particular form it took.

The dragon head’s fall is therefore not a story about a mistake disappearing. It remains a fall: an unstable object has disrupted the planned scene. What changes is the frame around that event. The actor acknowledges it, the prop is restored, and the scene resumes with the failure admitted into the performance. That response does not restore an untouched original. It makes a new continuation. In a play about a writer trying to give discarded characters a story, that small act of continuing carries its own weight. The prop’s material limit and the performers’ capacity to work with it become part of the story the production can tell.
